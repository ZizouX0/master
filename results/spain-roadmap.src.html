Why it has to be this way

Three constraints are fixed: 24 weeks (budget), no flight home, and B2 can only be earned in Spain — because Tunisia covers A1 and A2 only.

A 24-week course buys roughly six months of legal stay. That stay has to do two jobs, and the jobs sit far apart in the year.

Job 1 — deliver the certificate
B2 arrives at week 17. Add three weeks for the exam result. You cannot apply before week 21.
Job 2 — still be alive at the handover
The master's permit switches on a month before classes. Your permit must reach that date.

Why not start in September 2027

An autumn start delivers the certificate in January, in time for Fase 1 — the phase reserved for foreign-degree holders, with places held back and an admission list in February. It is the better door. But the course then ends in February and the permit dies in March, with the master seven months away.

Nothing legal fills that hole on this budget. A prórroga exists, but art. 55.5 ties it to a course you are enrolled in — you cannot buy bare time, only more weeks you cannot afford. The alternative is flying home, which is ruled out.

Why not start later than January 2028

Push the start to mid-February and the certificate arrives on 30 June — four days after the application window closes. The year is then lost.

It is the single week where both ends reach: the certificate clears the June deadline with ten days to spare, and the permit survives to 1 August — past the 22 July admission list.

The fortnight between the permits closes itself
Your permit ends 1 August; the master's begins 16 August. Filing on 24 July extends your legal status until the file is resolved, and the offices run about four months behind — Madrid's official figures for 1 January 2026 show them working on applications filed the previous September. So you remain legal straight through, and the new permit is already in force from 16 August. Here the backlog works for you.
Dates for 2028 are projected from the published 2026/27 cycle. The governing Acuerdo publishes in BOJA around 20–23 December 2027 — verify then and shift the start Monday if the window moves.

The one weak joint

Art. 54.3 wants the master's application filed two months before your current permit expires. Your permit expires 1 August, so the rule points at 2 June. But Fase 2's admission list does not exist until 22 July.

You would be filing 50 days later than the rule asks
This is anticipated. The DGGM Criterios Interpretativos (7 August 2025) §1 list accepted justifications, and yours is among them — the institution's own admission calendar making earlier filing impossible — proven by an official certificate or published calendar. §19 extends the same reasoning to this exact deadline.

But it is an argued exception, not a right. Late filing is formally a ground for inadmisión under LOEx Disposición Adicional 4ª. If refused, a recurso de reposición within one month cures the defect, and is reported to succeed on precisely this point.

Three free things that reduce the risk

  • Attach a university certificate stating the official Fase 2 dates. That specific document is what §1 asks for. A bare admission letter is weaker evidence.
  • Ask whether the university files under art. 54.6. If it does, resolution takes 15 days instead of four months. RD 316/2026, in force 16 April 2026, opened this to any officially recognised Spanish university. No university advertises it — you have to ask.
  • Write to the Oficina de Extranjería of the master's province now and ask how they treat a late filing justified by the DUA calendar. Get the answer before you commit money.

Two things that are settled in your favour
Sampere Alicante is on the Instituto Cervantes accredited list (Avda. Óscar Esplá 4) — that is the hard legal requirement of art. 52.1.e).2º, and it is met. And there is no “20 hours per week” rule: the phrase appears nowhere in RD 1155/2024, nowhere in the old RD 557/2011, and not once in Instrucción SEM 3/2025. It is consular folklore. 20 classes of 45 minutes is fine.

What this plan does not solve

Spain has no official master's degree in music production, electronic music, or sound design — the Ministry's register lists five official music master's nationally. Everything else sold under those names is título propio: not a degree. Spanish B2 opens acoustics engineering at about €821 a year in Andalucía, plus one twelve-place creative programme in Valencia with an unpublished entrance exam.

The best-fitting Spanish programme for a software-engineering graduate — UPF Barcelona's Sound and Music Computing — is taught in English and needs no Spanish at all. And every fully-funded route open to a Tunisian is English-taught: Fundación Carolina excludes Tunisia, AECID África-Med is civil-servants-only, Spanish university international scholarships are Ibero-American, and no scholarship anywhere funds a preparatory language year.

None of that makes this roadmap wrong. It makes it a plan you should choose deliberately rather than by default.
