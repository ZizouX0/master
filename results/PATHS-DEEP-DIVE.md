# Path 2 & Path 3, In Depth — and What a "Portfolio" Actually Means

For: Tunisian software-engineering graduate → goal: international DJ / music producer.
This document goes deep on the two paths closest to being an artist, and then explains the one
thing that gates almost every Path-2 application: **the portfolio**.

---

# PART 1 — PATH 2: SOUND DESIGN & PRODUCTION
### *You make the sound itself*

## What this field actually is

"Sound design and production" is not one job. It's a family of five, and most programs let you
lean toward one or two:

| Sub-field | What you actually do | Who pays you |
|---|---|---|
| **Music production** | Write, arrange, record, mix and master records | Artists, labels, yourself |
| **Sound design for picture** | Invent and build sounds for film, TV, animation | Studios, post houses |
| **Game audio** | Interactive sound — audio that reacts to what a player does | Game studios |
| **Post-production / mixing** | Dialogue, foley, balance, final mix, delivery specs | Post houses, studios |
| **Live electronics / performance** | Perform electronic music live; build your own instruments | Venues, festivals, yourself |

For your DJ/producer goal, **music production** is the core and **live electronics** is the
natural second. Game and film audio are where the reliable money is — worth keeping as a door.

## What you actually study

A two-year master typically runs like this:

**Semester 1 — foundations and levelling-up**
Critical listening (learning to actually *hear* what's wrong in a mix), studio recording
technique, signal flow, synthesis (subtractive, FM, granular, wavetable), sampling, DAW fluency
to a professional standard. You'll be pushed out of "bedroom producer" habits fast.

**Semester 2 — craft and specialisation**
Mixing and mastering in depth, arrangement and composition, sound design for a brief (you're
given a film scene or game level and must build its entire sound world), spatial/immersive audio
(Dolby Atmos, Ambisonics), and usually a collaborative project with film or game students.

**Semester 3 — professional practice**
A large project on your own terms, often an internship or an industry-facing commission. Portfolio
building becomes explicit. Many programs bring in working professionals to critique your work.

**Semester 4 — final project / thesis**
A substantial artistic work (an EP, a scored film, an installation, a live performance system)
plus written reflection explaining your artistic and technical decisions.

## The skill stack you walk out with

**Technical**
- Professional mixing and mastering — the difference between "sounds like a demo" and "sounds like a record"
- Synthesis and sound design from first principles: you can *build* a sound rather than hunt for a preset
- Recording technique: mic choice and placement, gain staging, room treatment
- Spatial audio, increasingly demanded by streaming and games
- DAW mastery — Ableton Live, Pro Tools, Reaper — plus Max/MSP and game middleware (Wwise, FMOD)

**Creative**
- Arrangement: how a track holds attention over 6 minutes
- A recognisable sonic identity — this is what actually distinguishes a producer
- Sound-to-picture storytelling: making sound carry emotion and narrative

**Professional**
- Session workflow, delivery specs, revision rounds, working to a client brief and deadline
- Collaboration with directors, artists, game designers
- The vocabulary to talk about sound with other professionals — underrated and career-defining

## What you end up doing

Music producer · mixing/mastering engineer · sound designer (film, TV, games, advertising) ·
audio director · technical sound designer (where your coding background becomes a superpower) ·
live-electronics performer · studio owner.

**Money reality, honestly:** this is mostly **freelance and project-based**. Income is lumpy —
strong months and empty months, especially in the first years. Game and film audio are the most
reliably salaried corners; pure music production is the least. Most working producers stack
several income streams (client work + sync licensing + their own releases + teaching). Plan for
this rather than being surprised by it.

## How it serves the DJ/producer goal

**This is the most direct path.** Concretely, it gives you:
- The craft to make your tracks competitive — most aspiring producers plateau on mixing, and this fixes exactly that
- Studio and equipment access you could not otherwise afford
- A **cohort of collaborators** — the single most underrated benefit; your classmates become your co-producers, your visual artists, the person who books you
- Two years living inside a European scene, legally, with time to play and meet people
- Critique from professionals — brutal, and the fastest way to improve

irregular. Go in knowing that.

# PART 2 — PATH 3: MUSIC BUSINESS
### *You build the business around the music*

## What this industry actually is

Before the degree, understand the money map — it's the thing most artists never learn, and it
costs them their careers:

**Every song is two separate assets.**
1. **The recording (the "master")** — the specific audio file. Usually owned by whoever paid for it: a label, or you.
2. **The composition (the "publishing")** — the underlying melody/lyrics/idea. Owned by the songwriters and their publisher.

When a track plays on Spotify or in a Netflix show, **both** get paid, separately, through
different pipelines. Artists who don't know this sign away one of them — often the more valuable one.

Around those two assets sit the industry's sectors:

| Sector | What it does | Roles |
|---|---|---|
| **Recorded music** | Labels, distribution, streaming strategy | A&R, label manager, digital strategy |
| **Publishing** | Manages song rights, collects royalties | Publisher, royalty analyst, sync agent |
| **Live** | Touring, booking, festivals, venues | Booking agent, tour manager, promoter |
| **Management** | Runs an artist's whole career | Artist manager |
| **Sync licensing** | Places music in film, games, ads (very lucrative) | Sync manager |
| **Brand / digital** | Partnerships, social, streaming marketing | Marketing manager |

## What you actually study

- **Copyright and IP law** — masters vs publishing, splits, moral rights, territory
- **Contracts** — recording, publishing, management, distribution, 360 deals; how to read one and where they hide the traps
- **Royalties and collection** — mechanicals, performance, neighbouring rights, PROs, and why money takes 18 months to arrive
- **Label and A&R operations** — how artists get signed, developed, and dropped
- **Streaming economics** — playlisting, algorithms, pro-rata vs user-centric payouts
- **Live and touring economics** — guarantees, splits, riders, why tours lose money
- **Marketing and artist development** — building an audience deliberately
- **Cultural policy and funding** — public grants and subsidies (large in Europe)

Assessment is essays, case studies, business plans, and usually an **industry internship** —
which is often where the actual job comes from.

## The skill stack you walk out with

- You can **read a contract** and spot what's being taken from you
- You understand **where every euro comes from** and how to claim it
- You can build a release strategy, a budget, and a marketing plan
- A network of industry contacts — this track is the most network-dense of the three
- Transferable business credentials that work outside music if you need them

## What you end up doing

Label roles · artist manager · booking agent · publisher / sync licensing · festival or venue
programmer · streaming-platform roles · tour manager · or **running your own label and artist
business**.

**Money reality:** more stable and salaried than Path 2, less than Path 1. Entry-level industry
roles pay modestly and are competitive; the money arrives with seniority and, especially, with a
roster or a catalogue you own.

## How it serves the DJ/producer goal

Indirectly but powerfully. It's the difference between an artist who gets exploited and one who
runs a business:
- You'd **self-manage** competently — negotiate your own splits, keep your publishing, register
  your works and actually collect
- You'd understand booking, so you can build a touring career rather than wait to be picked
- You could run a **small label** for yourself and peers — a very common route for electronic artists
- The internship and cohort put you inside the industry, next to bookers and labels

**But:** it teaches you to run the industry, not to make records. If your bottleneck is that
your tracks aren't good enough yet, this path won't fix it.

## Who this path is wrong for

If you want to spend your days making sound, you'll find this frustrating — it's spreadsheets,
contracts, essays and email. It's a great *second* skill and a risky *only* skill for someone
whose ambition is to be the artist.

# PART 3 — WHAT A "PORTFOLIO" ACTUALLY MEANS

This is the single thing that gates Path 2. Get it right and doors open; ignore it and no
amount of academic strength saves the application.

## What it is — and isn't

**A portfolio is evidence that you can make sound, and that you make deliberate decisions.**

It is **not** a CV, not a list of software you know, and not "everything I've ever made."
Admissions panels are practitioners. They listen for a few minutes and ask one question:
*does this person think like an artist, and can we teach them?*

Crucially: **they are not expecting professional-label quality.** They're selecting for
potential and intent. A rough but purposeful piece beats a polished but generic one.

## What your target programs actually ask for

These are the real published requirements from your shortlist:

| Program | The actual spec |
|---|---|
| **ReSound** | **Max 3 works, 10–15 minutes total**, each with a **reflective paragraph explaining your role** |
| **DIGICREA** | **Exactly 3–5 works**, none older than 5 years, with title, description and public links |
| **Sonology** | Compositions, sound installations, live-performance recordings, **or research results such as computer programs, articles, theses** — plus a **2-page study plan**. No live audition. |
| **HKU** | Portfolio showing **music creation and creative decision-making** |
| **CvA Immersive Audio** | **At least 3 stereo recordings in diverse styles**, then interview + on-the-spot Pro Tools mix test |
| **KCB Brussels** | CV, audio, video, scores, **Max patches, Ableton Live sessions** + live performance + interview |
| **REPLAY** | Max 5 projects in a 7-page PDF (template given) + **3-minute showreel** with a 30-second self-presentation |

Notice the pattern: **3–5 pieces, ~10–15 minutes, each explained.** That is the target.

## What counts as a "work"

Much broader than "a finished song." Any of these are legitimate:

1. **An original track** — the obvious one. Finished, mixed, intentional.
2. **A sound redesign** — take a film/game/trailer scene, strip its audio, rebuild it entirely.
   *Extremely common and highly respected* because it shows craft without needing you to compose.
3. **A sound installation or fixed-media piece** — a composed sonic environment, not a song.
4. **A live performance recording** — you performing electronic music, with video.
5. **A game-audio prototype** — interactive sound reacting to player input (Wwise/FMOD/Unity).
6. **An audio tool or instrument you built** — a Max patch, a plugin, a generative system, an
   ML audio experiment. **Sonology explicitly accepts computer programs. KCB explicitly accepts Max patches.**

## The reflective note — where most applicants lose

Every strong portfolio explains each piece in a short paragraph: **intent → your role → method →
honest reflection.** For example:

> **"Concrete Ground" (3:40, original track, 2026)**
> I wanted to build a piece where the rhythm comes entirely from non-musical sources, so I
> recorded metal, concrete and water around Tunis and used them as the whole percussion kit.
> I wrote, recorded, produced and mixed it alone. The samples were processed with granular
> synthesis in Ableton, using a Max patch I wrote to randomise grain position over time.
> The low end is the weak point — the sub competes with the kick — and it's what I most
> want to learn to fix.

Why this works: it shows a **concept**, your **exact role**, a **method** (with your technical
edge visible), and **self-critique** — which is the clearest signal that you're teachable.

## What kills applications

- ❌ Covers or remixes with no original contribution
- ❌ Loops and unfinished sketches — always submit *finished* pieces, however short
- ❌ 20 tracks that all sound the same — they asked for 3–5, obey it
- ❌ No explanation of what you actually did (especially in collaborations)
- ❌ Dead or private links — test every link from a logged-out browser
- ❌ Chasing polish over ideas — a distinctive rough piece beats a generic clean one

## Your unfair advantage

Most applicants to these programs are musicians who can't code. **You can.** Your database
shows this is explicitly valued:

- **Sonology** accepts *computer programs* as portfolio works and admits CS graduates outright
- **ReSound** names *computational science* as an accepted degree
- **KCB** accepts *Max patches and Ableton sessions* as portfolio material
- **DIGICREA** sits at the art/tech/science intersection by design

So do not hide the engineering — **make it the through-line.** A portfolio built around
"I build systems that generate and transform sound" is far more memorable than another
folder of house tracks. It gives you a *voice*, which is exactly what panels select for.

## A concrete 6-month build plan

You have until roughly **November 2026 – February 2027**. That's enough for a strong portfolio
of 4 pieces if you start now.

| Month | Build |
|---|---|
| **Aug 2026** | **Piece 1 — original track.** Finish something. Your best existing idea, completed and mixed properly. Finishing is the skill. |
| **Sep 2026** | **Piece 2 — sound redesign.** Pick a 90-second film/game/trailer scene, mute it, rebuild every sound. Shows craft fast. *(IELTS this month too.)* |
| **Oct 2026** | **Piece 3 — the code piece.** Your differentiator: a generative system, a Max patch, a custom instrument, or an ML audio experiment — plus a short piece made *with* it. |
| **Nov 2026** | **Piece 4 — original track #2, contrasting.** Different tempo/mood, to show range. Write the reflective notes for all four. |
| **Dec 2026** | Package it: host, write descriptions, build the PDF, get feedback, re-mix the weakest piece. |
| **Jan–Feb 2027** | Tailor per application (ReSound wants ≤15 min; DIGICREA wants 3–5 works) and submit. |

## How to package it

- **Host audio** on private/unlisted SoundCloud or Bandcamp; **video** on unlisted Vimeo/YouTube
- **One PDF** — for each piece: title, year, duration, role, the reflective paragraph, and the link
- **Name files properly**: `01_Concrete-Ground_original-track.pdf`, not `final_v3_REAL.wav`
- **Lead with your strongest piece.** Panels often stop after the first two.
- **Test every link logged out** before submitting

# PART 4 — CAN YOU DO BOTH?

Yes, and for your specific goal it's the smart play:

1. **MIAM Istanbul** offers *both* Sonic Arts / Sound Engineering **and** Music Business &
   Management concentrations, fully funded. One application, both worlds.
2. **Study Path 2, self-teach Path 3.** The business knowledge that matters most to a working
   artist — rights, splits, royalties, contracts — is genuinely learnable from books and courses.
   The craft in Path 2 is not; it needs studios, mentors and critique.
3. **Path 3 programs with an entrepreneurship angle** (Estonian Academy, Sibelius Global Music)
   are built for artists who run their own careers.

## My recommendation for you

**Lead with Path 2, and keep one funded Path-3 application as insurance.**

- **Primary:** ReSound → DIGICREA → HKU / Sonology *(Path 2, and your degree qualifies)*
- **Insurance:** MIAM Istanbul *(covers both tracks, fully funded)* and Estonian Academy
  *(any-degree entry, full tuition scholarship)*
- **Non-negotiable either way:** **IELTS by September 2026** and **4 portfolio pieces by December 2026**

Path 3 will always be learnable later, from books and from doing. Path 2's craft is what a
funded two years in a European studio buys you — and it's the thing that actually makes the
tracks good enough for the rest to matter.
