# 03 — *Carta de motivación y plan de estudios* (the document you sign)

This is the piece that does the job you described: it makes the officer see the language
course and the master's as **one plan**. It is entirely yours to write and sign — no
institution issues it, so there is nothing here that needs anyone else's letterhead.

## The two things it has to do at once

1. **Explain why the Spanish year is necessary** — not a holiday, not a way in. It is the
   prerequisite for the master's.
2. **Show you intend to leave when the studies end.** A study visa is a *stay*
   (`estancia`), not residence. This is the tension in your file: the master's plan proves
   your seriousness, but "I want to stay in Spain for years" reads as immigration intent.
   Resolve it by anchoring the **career after the studies to a specific outcome you return
   to or a mobile profession** — say what the qualification is *for*, and name what you go
   back to. Do not say you will look for work in Spain.

Keep it to **one page, maximum two.** Spanish, printed, signed, dated. No decoration.

## Notes on filling it in

**The paragraph that carries the letter** is the two-line itinerary block. It is the only
place where a reader sees both stages, with dates and hours, in one glance. Get the numbers
right and consistent with the two admission documents — a date that contradicts the school's
letter is worse than no letter at all.

**"Adjunto la comunicación de [UNIVERSIDAD]"** — only write this if you actually have the
reply from template [`01`](./01-email-preadmision-master.md). If you do not have it yet, cut
that sentence and write instead: *"He iniciado contacto con [UNIVERSIDAD] respecto a la
admisión para el curso 2027/28."* Never reference an attachment that is not in the envelope.

**On the return-intent paragraph.** Say what is true. If you have a job you are taking leave
from, a registered freelance activity, property, or dependants, name them — those are the
strongest single facts in a study-visa file. If you have none of them, do not invent any;
lean instead on the profession itself, which is genuinely location-independent, and on the
Spanish-language market argument you already made above.

**Language.** Write it in Spanish. If your Spanish is not yet good enough to write it
cleanly, have it checked by a native speaker or a sworn translator — but do not submit a
letter in an obviously machine-translated register, and do not submit it in French unless the
consulate's own checklist accepts French. A clumsy sentence is fine; a letter that reads as
not-yours is not.

**Keep a copy.** You will file a near-identical version a year later with the prórroga, and
it needs to tell the same story.
